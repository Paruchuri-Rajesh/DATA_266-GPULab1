# Task 1 failure analysis — Sivasurya Chandran

All snippets are copied exactly from `outputs/samples.txt`. They were generated by `checkpoints/gpt_final.pt` with 300 new characters each, using either greedy decoding or temperature 0.8.

The model writes text that reads like TinyStories locally: validation cross-entropy is 0.639 nats per character, and it predicts the next character correctly 79.6% of the time. Where it goes wrong is in the bigger picture: anything that happened more than one context window (128 characters) back.

## Case 1: Repetition loop (greedy decoding)

**Prompt:** `The sun was shining and`, greedy decoding, repeated 4-gram rate **0.841**

> The sun was shining and the sky was blue. The sun was shining and the sky was blue. The sun was shining and the sky was blue. The sun was shining and the sky was blue. The sun was shining and the sky was blue. The sun was shining and the sky was blue. The sun was shining and the sky was blue. The sun was shining and the s

**What goes wrong:** a degenerate repetition loop.

Greedy decoding always picks the single most likely next character. After "The sun was shining and the sky was blue.", the most likely continuation is to start that very common opening line again. Once the phrase fills the whole 128-character window, there's nothing else in the context to break the pattern, and it loops forever.

The decoding rule is to blame here, not the model. The same prompt at temperature 0.8 gives varied text, with repeated 4-gram rates between 0.000 and 0.014. Across all samples, the sampled outputs have a repeated 4-gram rate of 0.006, against 0.267 for greedy.

**Fix to test:** add a repetition penalty, or use top-k or nucleus sampling. Then check that the repeated 4-gram rate of the near-greedy outputs drops close to the sampled rate.

## Case 2: Losing track of who is who

**Prompt:** `Once upon a time`, temperature 0.8

> Once upon a time, there was a little boy named Timmy. Timmy loved to play outside and explore the world around him. One day, he saw a big rock in the park. "Look, Mommy! I found a shiny rock!" she said.
>
> "Mommy, look what I found!" said Jack.
>
> His mom looked up and saw a big, brown rocket that was very shiny and h

**What goes wrong:** loss of coherence (the characters and pronouns drift).

In about 200 characters, the main character goes from Timmy ("he") to "she" and then to Jack, and the rock turns into a rocket. Each sentence on its own is fine, and "rock" → "rocket" is a natural continuation for a character model because the two words start the same. The story just stops keeping track of who is who.

With a 128-character window, the model only sees the last sentence or two. By the time it writes the next speaker tag, "Timmy" has usually already dropped out of the context.

**Fix to test:** increase `block_size` to 256. Then count how often the main character's name changes per sample, before and after.

## Case 3: Broken grammar and events that make no sense

**Prompt:** `The sun was shining and`, temperature 0.8

> The sun was shining and the sky was grey. It was barking loudly and round and had a lot of fun.
>
> Suddenly, a big hole in the ground. He picked it up and took it home. When he got back home, he showed the ground to Sam.

**What goes wrong:** broken grammar and hallucinated events.

Several things go wrong at once:
- "Suddenly, a big hole in the ground." has no verb.
- "It was barking loudly" refers to nothing: the only thing "it" could be is the sky.
- "He" is never introduced.
- He "picked up" a hole.

The text keeps the rhythm of a TinyStories story, with short sentences, "Suddenly," and "When he got back home", but not the meaning. A character-level model learns spelling and local grammar well. It has very little sense of objects or how the world works, so sampling at T = 0.8 sometimes chains together phrases that are each plausible but add up to nonsense.

**Fix to test:** lower the temperature (say 0.6), or train a larger model for longer. To measure it, generate 50 samples for each setting and count how many sentences have no verb.
